Temperature and its Measurement

Key Concepts

"Wrong measurements are worse than no measurements at all."
— Anna Mani, Indian scientist (the "Weather Woman of India")
Big Idea

Hot or Cold? Touch is Tricky!

Take three bowls — warm, room temp, ice-cold water.

  • Dip your right hand in warm water
  • Dip your left hand in cold water
  • Wait 1–2 minutes
  • Then plunge BOTH into the middle (room temp) bowl
Your right hand feels cool, your left hand feels warm — but it's the SAME water! Touch is unreliable.
7.2

What is Temperature?

Since our senses are unreliable, we need a reliable measure of hotness.

Temperature is exactly that — a number telling us how hot or cold something is.

  • Hotter body → higher temperature
  • Colder body → lower temperature
A device that measures temperature is called a thermometer.
7.3.1

Clinical Thermometer (Body Temp)

The one your mother uses when you have fever — used to be mercury, now mostly digital.

  • Range: about 35°C to 42°C (human body range)
  • Place under tongue or in armpit
  • Wait for beep — read display
  • Wash tip with soap before and after
⚠️ Mercury thermometers are being replaced — mercury is highly toxic if it spills.
Body temp

37.0 °C — Normal

The "normal" body temperature is 37.0 °C — but this is just the AVERAGE.

  • Healthy people range: 35°C to 42°C
  • Small children: usually a bit higher
  • Older people: usually a bit lower
  • Time of day: lower in the morning, higher in evening
  • After exercise: temporarily higher
Armpit readings are 0.5°C to 1°C LOWER than the actual body temperature.

COVID era

Non-Contact (Infrared) Thermometer

Remember the gun-shaped thermometers used during COVID-19?

They measure temperature without touching the body — by sensing the infrared (heat) radiation it gives off.

  • No physical contact = no risk of spreading germs
  • Quick reading in seconds
  • Used at airports, malls, schools
7.3.2

Laboratory Thermometer

A long glass tube with a bulb at one end. Inside: a thin column of coloured alcohol or mercury.

  • Range: typically –10°C to 110°C
  • Can read ice (0°C) AND boiling water (100°C)
  • Liquid rises with heat, falls with cold
The reading is where the top of the liquid column meets the scale.
Use it Right

Correct Way to Use a Lab Thermometer

  1. Hold it vertically — not tilted
  2. Immerse the bulb in the liquid
  3. Don't let bulb touch the bottom or sides
  4. Wait till the liquid column stops moving
  5. Read while still immersed — taking it out changes the reading!
  6. Keep your eye level with the liquid surface
Range & Least Count

Range & Smallest Division

Two important facts about any thermometer:

  • Range: lowest to highest temperature it can measure
  • Least count: smallest temperature it can read (one tiny division)

Example: a thermometer with marks every 1°C, and 10 small lines between 0°C and 10°C → least count = 10°C ÷ 10 = 1°C.

7.3.3

Air Temperature

Room thermometers hang on walls of clinics, schools, hospitals — they tell us the room temperature.

Weather reports give the day's maximum and minimum air temperatures.

  • Air temp changes through the day
  • Rises in summer, falls in winter
  • Measured at weather stations around the world
Air temperature data + wind + humidity = weather forecasts!
Phase change

Why Boiling Water Stays at 100°C

Surprise: while water is BOILING, its temperature stays the SAME (~100°C in your area) — even though heat keeps coming in.

Similarly, while ice is MELTING, its temperature stays at 0°C.

The heat goes into changing the STATE (solid→liquid, liquid→gas), not into raising the temperature. You'll study this more in Grade 7!

⚠️ Why not

Why Not Mercury?

Old thermometers used mercury (silvery liquid metal). But it has problems:

  • Highly toxic if ingested or inhaled
  • If thermometer breaks → tiny mercury droplets are HARD to clean
  • Difficult to dispose of safely
Today, alcohol (coloured red) or digital sensors do the same job — safely.
Did you know?

Pulse Before Thermometers

Before thermometers were invented, how did people detect fever?

By checking the pulse rate — fever generally makes the heart beat faster.

This was known in ancient India!

But pulse rate alone isn't reliable — exercise, stress, and many other things also change it. A thermometer is the proper measure.

📘 From the Textbook

Activity 7.1 — The Three-Bowl Trick Easy

Materials Needed

  • 3 large bowls / containers (A, B, C)
  • Warm water (not hot!) for A
  • Tap (room temp) water for B
  • Ice-cold water for C
  • A timer

Procedure

  1. Set up the three bowls — A: warm, B: room temp, C: ice-cold.
  2. Dip your right hand in A and left hand in C. Wait 1-2 minutes.
  3. Pull both out and quickly dip them simultaneously into B.
  4. How does each hand feel?

Safety

Don't use scalding water for A. Warm = comfortable to touch.

What It Teaches

Right hand feels B as cool (because it was warmer). Left hand feels B as warm (because it was colder). Same water — two different feelings! Touch can't be trusted.

Activity 7.2 — Measure Body Temperature Easy

Materials Needed

  • A digital clinical thermometer
  • Soap & water (for cleaning)
  • A notebook
  • 10 willing friends or family members

Procedure

  1. Wash hands and the tip of the thermometer with soap.
  2. Reset the thermometer.
  3. Place the tip under the tongue with the mouth closed.
  4. Wait for the beep / flash.
  5. Read the display.
  6. Wash and dry the tip. Repeat for next person.
  7. Record all 10 readings.

Safety

Wash the tip with soap before AND after each use. Don't share without cleaning.

What It Teaches

Average might be 37°C — but actual readings vary from person to person. Body temperature is a RANGE, not a fixed number.

Activity 7.3 — Find the Thermometer's Range Easy

Materials Needed

  • A laboratory thermometer (from school lab)
  • A magnifying glass (if marks are tiny)

Procedure

  1. Look at the bottom of the scale. What's the lowest number?
  2. Look at the top of the scale. What's the highest?
  3. Range = highest − lowest.
  4. Note units (probably °C).

What It Teaches

Every thermometer has a different range. A clinical thermometer covers ~35–42°C (body range). A lab thermometer typically –10°C to 110°C (ice to boiling).

Activity 7.4 — Find the Least Count Medium

Materials Needed

  • A laboratory thermometer
  • A magnifying glass (helpful)
  • Pen & paper

Procedure

  1. Find two large marks close together (e.g., 0°C and 10°C).
  2. Calculate the difference: 10 − 0 = 10°C.
  3. Count the small divisions BETWEEN them.
  4. Divide: 10°C ÷ number of divisions = least count.

What It Teaches

Least count = smallest temperature change the thermometer can show. For ours: 10°C ÷ 10 divisions = 1°C.

Activity 7.5 — Measure Warm Water Correctly Medium

Materials Needed

  • Laboratory thermometer
  • Beaker of warm water (~50°C)
  • Clamp / stand (optional)

Procedure

  1. Pour warm water into the beaker.
  2. Dip the bulb of the thermometer into the water.
  3. Don't let the bulb touch the bottom or sides.
  4. Hold the thermometer vertical.
  5. Wait — watch the column rise. When it stops, read at eye level.
  6. Note the temperature WHILE the bulb is still in water.

Safety

Glass — handle carefully. Don't drop. Use warm, not boiling, water.

What It Teaches

Reading thermometers takes care! Reading from a tilt or after lifting out gives the wrong number. Eye level + immersed bulb = correct reading.

Activity 7.6 — Compare Boiling Water Readings Adult Help

Materials Needed

  • Beaker with water
  • Tripod stand + wire gauze
  • Burner / spirit lamp
  • Laboratory thermometer
  • Class of students 🙋

Procedure

  1. Adult heats water in the beaker until rolling boil.
  2. Each student takes a turn measuring the temperature.
  3. Record all readings.
  4. Compare — are they all the same?

Safety

Boiling water is dangerous. Adult demo only. Don't lean over the beaker.

What It Teaches

Readings differ slightly because of HOW students hold the thermometer, where the bulb is, eye level, etc. The temperature is the same — the technique matters!

Activity 7.7 — 10-Day Air Temperature Log Easy

Materials Needed

  • Notebook + pen
  • Daily weather report (newspaper / TV / phone weather app)
  • Optional: a room thermometer

Procedure

  1. For 10 successive days, find the max and min air temperature of your city.
  2. Note the date.
  3. Plot on a simple graph: days vs temperature.
  4. Look for patterns. Is it warming up? Cooling down?

What It Teaches

Air temperature changes daily — it's a weather variable. Patterns appear over weeks (e.g., as winter approaches). Real data, real science.

🎁 Bonus Add-Ons

Add-On 1 — Galileo's Bottle Thermoscope Medium

Materials Needed

  • An empty glass bottle
  • A drinking straw
  • Modelling clay or play-doh
  • A bit of coloured water
  • Hot water in a bowl, cold water in another

Procedure

  1. Fill the bottle ⅓ with coloured water.
  2. Insert the straw in the bottle so it dips into the water. Seal the bottle's mouth around the straw with clay (airtight).
  3. Place the bottle in hot water. The coloured water rises up the straw!
  4. Place in cold water. The level drops!

What It Teaches

Just like a real thermometer — but you built it yourself! Heat makes the air expand (rises liquid). Cold contracts it (falls). This is exactly how Galileo's original 1593 thermoscope worked.

Add-On 2 — Forehead vs Mouth vs Armpit Easy

Materials Needed

  • Digital clinical thermometer
  • Optional: infrared/forehead thermometer

Procedure

  1. Measure your temperature three ways: under tongue (1 min), under armpit (2 min), and at the forehead (if you have an IR thermometer).
  2. Compare the three readings.
  3. Mouth is usually the closest to "true" body temperature.

What It Teaches

Armpit reads 0.5-1°C lower than mouth. Forehead can vary with surrounding temperature. Doctors know this and adjust.

Add-On 3 — Convert °C to °F and K Easy

Materials Needed

  • A calculator (or your brain!)
  • Notebook

Procedure

  1. °C → °F: multiply by 9/5, then add 32.
  2. °C → K: add 273.15.
  3. Convert: 0°C, 37°C, 100°C, –10°C, 25°C into both °F and K.

What It Teaches

0°C = 32°F = 273.15 K. 37°C = 98.6°F = 310.15 K. 100°C = 212°F = 373.15 K. Three scales, one temperature.

Add-On 4 — Hottest/Coldest in My Home Easy

Materials Needed

  • A room / outdoor thermometer (or use a digital one carefully)
  • Notebook

Procedure

  1. Measure temperature in different rooms: kitchen, bedroom, bathroom, balcony, fridge (just hold close), freezer (very briefly!).
  2. Note when (day/night).
  3. Rank from hottest to coldest.

What It Teaches

Different rooms have different temperatures, depending on sunlight, ventilation, heating sources. The kitchen during cooking is hot; the freezer is way below 0°C.

Add-On 5 — Body Temp Pattern Over a Day Easy

Materials Needed

  • Digital thermometer
  • Notebook
  • One full day

Procedure

  1. Take your temperature on waking up.
  2. Take it before breakfast, mid-morning, afternoon, evening, before sleeping.
  3. Plot a chart.

What It Teaches

Body temperature is usually LOWEST in the early morning and HIGHEST in the late afternoon/evening. There's a daily rhythm — even when you're perfectly healthy.

Add-On 6 — Make a Personal Weather Station Medium

Materials Needed

  • Outdoor thermometer (place in shade!)
  • A notebook
  • 2 weeks

Procedure

  1. Place the thermometer in a shaded outdoor spot (don't put it in direct sun).
  2. Note the temperature at 9 AM, noon, 3 PM, and 6 PM each day for 2 weeks.
  3. Plot the highs and lows.
  4. Compare with your weather app.

What It Teaches

You're now doing what Anna Mani did at scale. Real weather data, collected by you. Highs are usually 2-3 PM, lows are before dawn.

Add-On 7 — Boiling Water Stays at 100°C Adult Help

Materials Needed

  • Beaker
  • Burner / kettle
  • Lab thermometer
  • Stopwatch

Procedure

  1. Heat water gently. Note temperature every minute.
  2. Watch — temperature rises until ~100°C.
  3. Now keep heating. Temperature STAYS at 100°C even though heat keeps adding!
  4. The extra heat is going into making steam.

Safety

Adult supervision. Hot water + steam can scald. Don't lean over.

What It Teaches

During a phase change (liquid → gas), all the heat goes into the change of state — not into temperature. This is why steam burns more than hot water does.
