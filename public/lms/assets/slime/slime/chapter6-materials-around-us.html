Materials Around Us

Key Concepts

Big Idea

What is a Material?

Look at your school bag, your books, your pen, your tiffin box, your water bottle…

Every object is made of one or more materials — like paper, wood, cloth, glass, metal, plastic, clay.

Any substance used to make an object is called a material.
6.2

Why Group Materials?

Arranging objects into groups is called classification.

Why do we classify?

  • It helps us see patterns
  • It helps us find things (like spices in the kitchen)
  • It helps us choose the right material for each purpose
You can group materials by colour, shape, hardness, shine, transparency, or what they are made of.
6.3.1

Lustre — Shiny or Dull?

Some materials shine when light falls on them. We say they have lustre.

  • Lustrous ✨: iron, copper, aluminium, gold, silver (usually metals)
  • Non-lustrous: paper, wood, rubber, jute
⚠️ "All that glitters is not gold!" Some non-metals are coated with shiny plastic or wax to look lustrous — but they're not metals.
6.3.2

Hard or Soft?

Press it. Scratch it.

  • Hard materials don't compress or scratch easily: stone, iron, brick
  • Soft materials compress easily: sponge, cotton, rubber, pillow
"Hard" is relative — rubber is harder than sponge but softer than iron.
6.3.3

Transparent / Translucent / Opaque

Can you see through it?

TypeMeansExamples
TransparentSee clearlyGlass, water, air, cellophane
TranslucentSee — but not clearlyButter paper, frosted glass
OpaqueCannot see at allWood, cardboard, metal, walls
6.3.4

Soluble or Insoluble?

Drop it in water and stir:

  • Soluble ✓ — disappears completely: sugar, salt, lemon juice
  • Insoluble ✗ — doesn't dissolve: sand, chalk powder, sawdust
Water dissolves many things — that's why it's important for our bodies and for fish (oxygen also dissolves in water!).

Some liquids mix with water (vinegar, lemon juice) and some don't (oil, honey can settle).

6.3.5

Mass — How Much Matter?

An iron ball is heavier than a wooden ball of the same size. Why?

Because they have different mass.

Mass tells us the amount of matter in an object.

  • Measured with a balance
  • Unit: kilogram (kg) or gram (g)
1 kg = 1000 g. Always leave a space between number and unit: write 7 kg, not 7kg or 7kgs.
6.3.6

Volume — How Much Space?

Volume = the space a substance occupies.

  • A bag on your seat takes up space → you can't sit there!
  • A 1 L bottle of water has more volume than a 500 mL bottle

Measured in: litre (L), millilitre (mL), or cubic metre (m³).

1 m³ = 1000 L.
6.4

What is Matter?

Two special properties are shared by EVERY material:

  • It has mass
  • It occupies volume (space)

Anything that has mass AND occupies space is called matter.

All materials are matter. Water, sand, air, cup, pencil — all matter! Even air has mass (very little, but real).
India

🏺 Ancient Pottery

The earliest pottery in India dates back 7,000–8,000 years!

  • Found in Lahuradewa (Ganga plains) and Mehrgarh (Baluchistan)
  • Around 4000 BCE — wheel-turned pottery, painted designs, "slips" (coloured coats)
  • Sindhu-Sarasvatī (Harappan) Civilisation: bright red surfaces, black designs
  • Terracotta = baked clay
Large storage jars and other Harappan pottery are exhibited at the National Museum, New Delhi.

Real life

💧 Make ORS at Home

ORS = Oral Rehydration Solution — used to treat dehydration from diarrhoea.

6 tsp sugar + ½ tsp salt
in 1 litre boiled, cooled water

The sugar and salt are soluble in water. Once dissolved, the body can absorb them quickly. A simple chemistry that saves lives!

🧪 Lab Experiments — Try These at Home / In Class

All textbook activities + bonus add-ons. EasyMediumAdult Help

📘 From the Textbook

Activity 6.1 — Identify Materials Around You Easy

Materials Needed

  • A notebook + pen
  • Your eyes & your home / classroom

Procedure

  1. Walk around your home or classroom.
  2. List 10 objects you see in your notebook.
  3. Next to each, write what material(s) it's made of.
  4. Some objects use MORE than one material (e.g., a pen = plastic + metal + ink).

What It Teaches

Everything around us is made of materials. The same material can be used for many objects (plastic → bottles, pens, toys, chairs).

Activity 6.4 — Bouncing Balls Easy

Materials Needed

  • Tennis ball
  • Cricket ball
  • Hand-exercise ball (rubber stress ball)
  • Tape measure or ruler
  • A wall for marking heights

Procedure

  1. Mark a height (about 1 metre) on the wall.
  2. Hold each ball at that height. Drop one at a time.
  3. Watch how high it bounces back. Mark each.
  4. Rank: highest bounce → lowest bounce.

What It Teaches

Same size — different materials → different bounce. Rubber bounces more than cork (cricket ball) more than dense rubber filling. Material decides the use!

Activity 6.5 — Hard or Soft? Easy

Materials Needed

  • A brick
  • A water bottle
  • A pillow
  • A glass tumbler
  • A wooden table
  • A sweater (wool/cotton)

Procedure

  1. Press each object with your finger.
  2. Does it dent / squish? It's SOFT.
  3. Does it stay firm? It's HARD.
  4. Write hard/soft + the material it's made of in your table.

What It Teaches

Hardness is relative. Brick → very hard. Pillow → very soft. We choose materials based on whether we need hard or soft (chair top: hard. Cushion: soft.)

Activity 6.6 — See-Through Test Easy

Materials Needed

  • Glass tumbler
  • Butter paper / tracing paper
  • An eraser
  • Frosted glass (or a piece of frosted plastic)
  • Wooden board
  • Window glass
  • A torch / phone light

Procedure

  1. Hold each item up to a light source.
  2. Can you see clearly through it? → TRANSPARENT
  3. Can you see a hazy outline? → TRANSLUCENT
  4. Can't see at all? → OPAQUE
  5. Now classify each item.

What It Teaches

Light passes through different materials in different amounts. That's why we use glass for windows (transparent), butter paper for tracing (translucent), wood for doors (opaque).

Activity 6.7 — What Dissolves in Water? Easy

Materials Needed

  • 5 clear glass tumblers
  • Water (fill each ⅔)
  • Sugar, salt, chalk powder, sand, sawdust (small amounts of each)
  • 5 separate spoons

Procedure

  1. Fill 5 tumblers ⅔ with water.
  2. Add 1 spoon of each substance to its own tumbler.
  3. Predict what will happen for each — dissolve or not.
  4. Stir each well. Wait 2 minutes.
  5. Observe — note which disappear (soluble) and which stay (insoluble).

What It Teaches

Sugar, salt → soluble. Chalk powder, sand, sawdust → insoluble (they may float or settle). This is why we can make sweet lemonade but can't drink water with sand in it!

Activity 6.8 — Heavy or Light? Easy

Materials Needed

  • 3 identical paper cups (or small bowls)
  • Water
  • Sand
  • Pebbles
  • A balance (or just compare by hand)

Procedure

  1. Fill each cup half-way: A with water, B with sand, C with pebbles.
  2. Predict order: heaviest → lightest.
  3. Place on a balance, two at a time.
  4. Watch the balance tip!

What It Teaches

Same volume — different mass. Pebbles are usually heaviest, then sand, then water. Mass tells us how much "stuff" is packed in.

🎁 Bonus Add-Ons

Add-On 1 — Make ORS at Home Easy

Materials Needed

  • 1 litre of boiled, cooled water
  • 6 teaspoons sugar
  • ½ teaspoon salt
  • A clean bottle

Procedure

  1. Boil water for 5 min. Let it cool.
  2. Pour 1 L into the bottle.
  3. Add 6 teaspoons sugar + ½ teaspoon salt.
  4. Stir until completely dissolved (try the tongue test: it should taste like tears).

Safety

Only drink it if needed (diarrhoea / dehydration). Get adult guidance — overuse can cause salt imbalance.

What It Teaches

This is the same ORS that saves millions of lives every year. The sugar & salt are SOLUBLE in water — once dissolved, the body absorbs them quickly.

Add-On 2 — Make a Rainbow with Sugar Layers Medium

Materials Needed

  • 5 glasses
  • Sugar (lots — about 10 spoons)
  • Food colouring (4 different colours)
  • Spoon (for gentle pouring)
  • Tall narrow glass for the final stack

Procedure

  1. In glass 1: 4 tsp sugar + warm water (most concentrated) + red colour.
  2. In glass 2: 3 tsp sugar + warm water + yellow.
  3. In glass 3: 2 tsp sugar + warm water + green.
  4. In glass 4: 1 tsp sugar + warm water + blue.
  5. SLOWLY pour glass 1 into the tall glass.
  6. Then VERY slowly pour glass 2 over the back of a spoon. It floats on top!
  7. Repeat with 3 and 4. You'll see beautiful layers.

What It Teaches

More sugar = heavier (denser) solution. Less dense floats on more dense. Same idea explains why oil floats on water!

Add-On 3 — Penny Polish (Lustre vs Tarnish) Easy

Materials Needed

  • 5-10 old dull copper coins
  • Lemon juice or vinegar
  • Pinch of salt
  • Small bowl

Procedure

  1. Pour vinegar into the bowl. Add a pinch of salt. Stir.
  2. Drop in dull coins. Wait 30 sec, swirl.
  3. Take out, dry with cloth.
  4. Shiny copper! Lustre revealed.

What It Teaches

The dullness on coins is a thin coating of copper oxide. Acid + salt strips it off, revealing the original shiny metal — its true lustre.

Add-On 4 — Air Has Mass! Medium

Materials Needed

  • 2 identical balloons
  • A long ruler or stick
  • Thread
  • A pin

Procedure

  1. Tie a balloon to each end of the stick. Balance the stick from the centre with a thread.
  2. Now inflate ONE balloon and re-tie.
  3. The stick TILTS toward the inflated balloon.
  4. Why? Because the air inside has mass!

What It Teaches

We don't usually think of air as having mass — but it does (just very little). Anything that has mass and takes up space is MATTER. So air IS matter!

Add-On 5 — Window Spotter: Which is Translucent? Easy

Materials Needed

  • A torch / phone light
  • Things from around: cling film, fabric, paper, plastic sheets, leaves, etc.

Procedure

  1. In a darkish room, hold each item against the torch.
  2. Sort into 3 piles based on what you see through it.
  3. Take photos for fun comparisons!

What It Teaches

Many everyday materials are translucent — not fully clear, not fully opaque. Frosted glass, tissue paper, butter paper, white plastic bags.

Add-On 6 — Volume by Displacement Medium

Materials Needed

  • A measuring cup or graduated jar
  • Water
  • A small stone, a small toy, a metal key

Procedure

  1. Fill the measuring cup with 200 mL water. Note the level exactly.
  2. Drop in the stone. Note the new water level.
  3. The difference is the volume of the stone!
  4. Repeat for other objects.

What It Teaches

You can measure volume even for funny-shaped things — by seeing how much water they push aside. (This is what Archimedes did, in his famous bathtub story!)

Add-On 7 — Material Detective Easy

Materials Needed

  • A notebook
  • 10 items you find around the house

Procedure

  1. For each item, fill out:
    • Lustrous or non-lustrous?
    • Hard or soft?
    • Transparent / Translucent / Opaque?
    • Soluble in water?
    • Made of what materials?
  2. Compare two items. Which has more properties in common?

What It Teaches

Every object is a unique combination of properties — and putting them in a table makes patterns suddenly visible. That's scientific classification in action!

Add-On 8 — Ayurveda Property Match Easy

Materials Needed

  • Just a thinking cap! And maybe a snack.

Procedure

  1. Look at the 10 Ayurveda property pairs (in Concepts tab).
  2. Pick 5 objects around you (rice, ice, oil, brick, cloud).
  3. For each, try to assign which guṇa applies (e.g., ice = hima (cold), kaṭhina (hard), shlakṣhana (smooth)…).

What It Teaches

Ancient Indian thinkers had a sophisticated way to describe ANY material — using just 10 pairs of opposites. Compare with our modern 5–6 properties. Both work!
