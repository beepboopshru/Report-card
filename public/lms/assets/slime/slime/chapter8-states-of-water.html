A Journey through States of Water

Key Concepts

"If it does not rain well, even the mighty ocean will be drained."
— Thirukkural (ancient Tamil text)
Big Idea

Same Substance, Three Faces 🧊💧☁️

Ice feels hard. Water flows. Water vapour is invisible. Could they really be the same thing?

YES! They are three states of the SAME substance — H₂O.

  • 🧊 Ice — solid state
  • 💧 Water — liquid state
  • ☁️ Water vapour — gaseous state
Take an ice cube out of the freezer → wait → it melts to water. Same substance, just changed state.
8.1

Evaporation 💧→☁️

Wet clothes dry. Puddles disappear. Water on a hot pan vanishes.

Evaporation is the process of water turning into water vapour — even at room temperature.

  • Drying of mopped floor
  • Sweat on our body dries up
  • Hand sanitiser feels cool as it evaporates
Water vapour is invisible! Steam from a kettle is visible only because of tiny droplets, not the vapour itself.
8.2

Condensation ☁️→💧

Why do water drops form on the outside of a glass of cold water? The water didn't seep through!

Condensation is water vapour from the air turning back into liquid when it touches a cold surface.

  • Dew on plants in the morning 🌿
  • Water under a steel plate on a boiling pot
  • Fog on a cold window
  • Clouds in the sky (giant condensation!)
Experiment

Did Water Seep Through? Or Form from Air?

The "weighing balance test" (Activity 8.4) settles it:

  1. Put glass of cold water on a digital balance
  2. Wait 30 minutes, observing mass every 5 min
  3. Mass INCREASES as droplets form outside!
Mass can't appear from nowhere. The extra water came from the AIR around (water vapour) → condensation!
8.3

Other substances also have states: wax, ghee, oil can be solid or liquid depending on temperature.
8.4

The Big Four State Changes

  • Melting: Solid → Liquid (heat) — e.g. ice melts
  • Freezing: Liquid → Solid (cool) — water freezes
  • Evaporation: Liquid → Gas (heat) — water → vapour
  • Condensation: Gas → Liquid (cool) — vapour → droplets
Pattern: heat = one direction, cool = the other direction. State changes are reversible!
8.5

What Makes Evaporation FASTER?

Four factors:

  • Bigger surface area — plate > bottle cap
  • Higher temperature — sun > shade, hot day > cold day
  • More wind — windy > still air
  • Lower humidity — dry air > humid air
That's why clothes dry faster on a hot, windy, sunny day — and slower on a humid rainy day.
8.6

Cooling Effect of Evaporation

When water evaporates, it takes heat away with it. Things left behind get cooler!

  • Matka / Surahi: water seeps through clay, evaporates, cools the water inside
  • Sweating: body cools as sweat evaporates
  • Sanitiser: feels cold on hands
  • Sprinkling water on floor: cools the house in summer
Why we feel cooler under a fan: the wind speeds up sweat evaporation!
India

🏺 Pot-in-Pot Cooler

An ancient Indian invention — no electricity needed!

Two earthen pots, one inside the other, with wet sand between. As water evaporates from the sand, it cools the inner pot — keeping vegetables fresh for days!

Also called a Zeer pot. Used today by farmers in regions without refrigerators.
8.7

Clouds & Rain ☁️→☔

How does ocean water end up falling on your school?

  1. Evaporation: Sun heats ocean — water becomes vapour
  2. Rising: Water vapour is lighter than air, so it goes up
  3. Cooling: High up, the air is cool
  4. Condensation: Vapour forms droplets around dust particles
  5. Cloud: Droplets float together as clouds
  6. Rain: Droplets join and grow heavy → fall as rain (or snow/hail)
Water Cycle

The Endless Journey 🔄

Water never disappears — it just cycles:

Ocean → Evaporates → Clouds → Rain → Rivers → Ocean → repeat 🔁

Same drops of water have been cycling for billions of years. The water you drink today may have once been in a dinosaur 🦕!

AWG

Atmospheric Water Generator 💦

Modern machines actually COLLECT water from the air!

They cool down humid air → water vapour condenses → drinkable water.

Useful in dry regions where rain is scarce. Same principle as a glass of ice water in summer (just on a bigger scale).

Conserve

Why Save Water?

Only a tiny fraction of Earth's water is drinkable:

  • ~97% is salty ocean water (can't drink)
  • ~2% is frozen in glaciers
  • Less than 1% is the fresh water we use!
⚠️ With population growing, water shortages are real. Don't waste water. Don't pollute rivers/lakes!

📖 A Hot Afternoon with Swara, Ahana & Amma

A bright Sunday afternoon in May. The fans whirr above. Swara (age 11) and her younger sister Ahana (age 9) sprawl on the cool floor.

Swara: Amma! I'm SO thirsty. And it's so hot today.

Amma walks in with a brass tray and two glasses of shikanji from the kitchen.

Amma: Here, beta — fresh shikanji from the matka.

Ahana: (takes a sip) Mmm! This is SO cold! But Amma — there's no fridge in the kitchen. How is the water cold?

Amma: Good question! The water seeps a tiny bit through the porous clay of the matka. From outside, it slowly turns into water vapour — that's called evaporation. And here's the clever part: evaporation takes heat away with it. So the matka has a cooling effect on the water inside.

Swara: (holding her glass) Amma… look! The OUTSIDE of my glass is wet. Is it leaking?

Amma: Not at all. The air around us has tiny invisible particles of water floating in it — we call that water vapour. When the water vapour touches a cold surface like your cold glass, it turns back into liquid droplets. That's called condensation.

Ahana: So the droplets came from the AIR?? Not from inside the glass?

Amma: Exactly! You can prove it — mark the level inside, weigh the glass, wait an hour. The level inside won't change, but the glass will be heavier. Magic? No — just science.

The kettle on the stove starts whistling. White steam streams from its spout.

Swara: What's the white cloud coming out of the kettle?

Amma: That's steam — actually it's tiny droplets of water in the air. The hot water inside has turned into water vapour and is rushing out. Going from liquid to gas is called evaporation, like I told you.

Ahana: Oooh! Like our wet clothes drying in the sun!

Amma: Yes! Same idea.

Amma walks into the kitchen and opens the freezer — and gasps.

Amma: Oh no, I left the door slightly open. Come see, girls.

Inside, the ice tray is half-melted — solid cubes have collapsed into a small pool.

Swara: The ice became water!

Amma: Yes — going from solid to liquid is called melting. If I close the freezer tightly now, the water will become hard ice again — that\'s called freezing.

Ahana: Wait — so ice, water, and water vapour are all the same thing?

Amma: Yes! They\'re all the same H₂O — just in different states. Ice is the solid state, water is the liquid state, water vapour is the gaseous state.

Ahana runs to the window. Clouds float over the colony.

Ahana: What about clouds, Amma? Are they water too?

Amma: Yes! Up high where the air is cool, water vapour condenses around tiny dust particles — and the droplets float as clouds. When droplets get heavy, they fall as rain (or snow or hail). The whole journey — ocean → vapour → cloud → rain → river → ocean — is called the water cycle.

Swara: (slowly, looking at her glass) So this water in my shikanji… has probably been ocean water, cloud, rain — over and over again?

Amma: Yes! The same water has been cycling for billions of years. The water you\'re drinking today may have once been drunk by a dinosaur!

The sisters look at each other, then at their glasses. Then they take a long, slow sip.

Ahana: (grinning) Whoaaa.

🧊 Experiment 1: Ice Cube on a Warm Table (5 min)

What you need: 1 ice cube, a small saucer.

Do this: Place the ice in the saucer on the table. Watch every minute for 20 minutes.

What to observe: Solid → liquid. The same substance, different state. Concept: melting.

💧 Experiment 2: Water on a Plate (1 hour)

What you need: 1 tablespoon of water, a steel plate, a sunny window or warm room.

Do this: Pour the water onto the plate. Place near sunlight. Check every 15 min. The plate doesn\'t leak — but the water keeps shrinking.

What to observe: Liquid disappearing into thin air. Concept: evaporation.

🥶 Experiment 3: Wet Cold Glass (5 min)

What you need: A glass, cold tap water, 2-3 ice cubes.

Do this: Fill glass with cold water + ice. Wait 5 minutes. Touch the outside — wet!

What to observe: The water came from the AIR, not the glass. Concept: condensation.

📘 From the Textbook

Activity 8.1 — Watch Ice Melt Easy

Materials Needed

  • An ice cube
  • A clean dry cup
  • A timer

Procedure

  1. Place the ice cube in the cup.
  2. Leave it on the table at room temperature.
  3. Observe at 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 minutes.
  4. Note when it has fully melted into water.

What It Teaches

Ice (solid H₂O) melts into water (liquid H₂O). Same substance — different state. The cup doesn't change weight (nothing added or removed)!

Activity 8.2 — Water on a Plate Easy

Materials Needed

  • Steel plate
  • 1 tablespoon of water

Procedure

  1. Pour a tablespoon of water onto a clean steel plate.
  2. Check: does water leak underneath?
  3. Leave for a few hours.
  4. Observe — water level decreases, eventually empty.

What It Teaches

Water didn't seep through (steel is solid). It EVAPORATED into the air. This proves evaporation happens at room temperature.

Activity 8.3 — Cold Glass Mystery Easy

Materials Needed

  • Glass tumbler
  • Cold water
  • A few ice cubes

Procedure

  1. Pour cold water into the glass.
  2. Add ice cubes.
  3. Leave undisturbed for 5 minutes.
  4. Look at the outer surface of the glass.
  5. Touch it — what do you feel?

What It Teaches

Water droplets form on the OUTSIDE of the glass. They came from water vapour in the air, condensing on the cold surface.

Activity 8.4 — Weighing Balance Test Medium

Materials Needed

  • Digital weighing balance
  • Glass tumbler half-filled with cold water + ice
  • Small steel plate to cover
  • Marker to mark water level inside
  • Timer

Procedure

  1. Mark the water level inside the glass with a marker.
  2. Cover the glass with the steel plate.
  3. Place on the balance. Note the mass at 0 min.
  4. Wait. Every 5 min, note the mass — for 30 min.
  5. Check: water level inside should be unchanged. But mass should INCREASE.

What It Teaches

Internal water level stays — so water didn't seep out. Mass increases — extra water came from the air via condensation. Proof!

Activity 8.5 — Three States Compare Easy

Materials Needed

  • 2 containers of different shapes
  • An ice cube
  • A cup of water
  • An open room (for water vapour)

Procedure

  1. Put the ice cube in container 1, then container 2. Does its shape change?
  2. Pour water from container 1 to 2. Does its shape change?
  3. Pour water on a clean surface. Watch it spread.
  4. Imagine water vapour — how would it behave? (It spreads to fill all available space!)

What It Teaches

Solid (ice) — fixed shape. Liquid (water) — takes container shape, fixed volume. Gas (vapour) — fills all space, no shape, no fixed volume.

Activity 8.7 — Surface Area Affects Evaporation Easy

Materials Needed

  • Bottle cap (small)
  • Plate (large, flat)
  • Same amount of water in each (e.g., 1 spoonful)
  • Timer

Procedure

  1. Pour same amount of water in the cap and the plate.
  2. Keep them side by side (same temperature, same place).
  3. Observe every 30 min.
  4. Note which evaporates first.

What It Teaches

Plate evaporates much faster. More surface area = more contact with air = more evaporation per minute.

Activity 8.8 — Sunlight vs Shade Easy

Materials Needed

  • 2 identical bottle caps
  • Same amount of water in each
  • A sunny spot
  • A shaded spot
  • Timer

Procedure

  1. Fill each cap with same water amount.
  2. Place one in sunlight, one in shade.
  3. Check every 15 min.
  4. Note when each one becomes dry.

What It Teaches

Sunlight-cap empties much faster. Higher temperature → faster evaporation. Same applies to windy day, dry air.

Activity 8.9 — Build a Pot-in-Pot Cooler Medium

Materials Needed

  • 2 earthen pots (one bigger, one smaller — smaller fits inside)
  • Sand (enough to fill the gap)
  • Water
  • Wet jute sack OR a lid
  • Vegetables / fruits to test

Procedure

  1. Layer the bottom of the big pot with sand.
  2. Place the small pot inside.
  3. Fill the gap between with more sand.
  4. Pour water into the sand to make it wet.
  5. Cover the small pot with a lid or wet jute sack.
  6. Place vegetables inside the small pot.
  7. Add water to the sand daily — keep moist.
  8. Check freshness after 5–7 days.

What It Teaches

As water evaporates from the wet sand, it draws heat away — cooling the inner pot. Vegetables stay fresh for days without electricity!

Activity 8.10 — Make a Cloud in a Bottle Adult Help

Materials Needed

  • Empty 1-litre plastic bottle
  • ~1 cup of water
  • A small piece of newspaper
  • Matches (adult only)

Procedure

  1. Pour ~1 cup of water into the bottle.
  2. Close the lid. Squeeze and release the bottle quickly for 2-3 minutes. Look inside — any cloud?
  3. Open. Adult lights a piece of newspaper, blows it out, then drops the smoking paper inside.
  4. Close tightly. Squeeze and release again.
  5. Now — a hazy CLOUD appears!

Safety

Adult handles fire. Don't keep flame near the bottle. Have water ready.

What It Teaches

Water vapour + dust particles (from smoke) + cool air (from sudden release of pressure) → tiny droplets = cloud! Same physics as a real cloud in the sky.

Activity 8.11 — Label the Water Cycle Easy

Materials Needed

  • Chart paper
  • Markers / crayons
  • Reference: pictures of clouds, ocean, river, etc.

Procedure

  1. Draw the Earth's surface, ocean, mountains, river.
  2. Draw the sun and clouds.
  3. Add arrows showing: Evaporation (ocean up), Condensation (vapour to cloud), Rain (cloud down), River flow (back to ocean).
  4. Label each part clearly.

What It Teaches

The complete water cycle. Drawing it helps you visualize where every drop of water goes.

🎁 Bonus Add-Ons

Add-On 1 — Mini Water Cycle in a Jar Easy

Materials Needed

  • Glass jar with lid (or clingfilm)
  • Warm water (~3 cm in jar)
  • 3-4 ice cubes

Procedure

  1. Pour warm water in the jar.
  2. Seal with lid (or clingfilm).
  3. Place ice on top of the lid.
  4. Wait 5–10 min. Observe.

What It Teaches

Warm water evaporates → vapour hits cold lid → condenses → droplets rain back into water. Complete water cycle in your jar!

Add-On 2 — Hot Hand vs Cold Hand Sanitiser Test Easy

Materials Needed

  • Hand sanitiser

Procedure

  1. Put a small amount on your palm.
  2. Notice how it FEELS as it dries — cool, right?
  3. Try wafting air over it with your other hand — feels even cooler!

What It Teaches

Sanitiser (mostly alcohol) evaporates quickly, taking heat away from your skin. That's why sanitisers feel cool. Same as sweating + fan.

Add-On 3 — Make Rain by Boiling Adult Help

Materials Needed

  • Saucepan
  • Water
  • Stovetop
  • A cold metal plate / lid

Procedure

  1. Adult heats water in the pan.
  2. Before water boils, hold a cold metal plate over the steam (from above, not touching).
  3. Watch — droplets form on the plate's underside.
  4. The droplets join → fall like raindrops back into the pan!

Safety

Steam burns are nasty. Adult holds the plate at a safe distance. Cold plate handle wrapped in cloth.

What It Teaches

Evaporation from heated water → vapour rises → cold plate → condensation → "rain" falls back down. The water cycle in 60 seconds!

Add-On 4 — Wet Towel Cool Fridge Easy

Materials Needed

  • A water bottle
  • A wet cotton towel
  • A sunny / windy outdoor spot

Procedure

  1. Wrap the water bottle in the wet towel.
  2. Place outside in a breezy spot.
  3. After 1 hour, feel the bottle. Cool!

What It Teaches

Water in the towel evaporates, taking heat away — keeping the bottle cool. Cousin of the matka. Used in camping and deserts for centuries.

Add-On 5 — Footprints of Evaporation Easy

Materials Needed

  • A wet finger
  • A blackboard / dry counter

Procedure

  1. Wet your finger lightly.
  2. Draw your name on a dry surface.
  3. Watch.
  4. Your "name" will disappear from one end first.

What It Teaches

Evaporation happens silently and steadily. Even from a thin film of water. Where you wrote thicker, it lasts longer.

Add-On 6 — Track Daily Humidity Easy

Materials Needed

  • A weather app on your phone
  • Notebook

Procedure

  1. Each day for 2 weeks, check the humidity number on your weather app.
  2. Also note: was it sunny, rainy, windy?
  3. How fast do clothes dry on each day?

What It Teaches

Humidity = water vapour already in the air. High humidity (like rainy days) = clothes dry slowly. Dry, windy days = humidity low, clothes dry fast.

Add-On 7 — Salt Water vs Fresh Water Evaporation Easy

Materials Needed

  • 2 small plates
  • 1 spoon fresh water + 1 spoon salt water (same amount)

Procedure

  1. Put fresh water on one plate, salt water on another.
  2. Leave both in a sunny spot.
  3. Once both have evaporated, look closely at each plate.
  4. One has nothing. The other has white crystals (salt) left behind!

What It Teaches

Only water evaporates — not the salt. This is how sea salt is harvested! The ocean evaporates, salt stays.

Add-On 8 — Mini Greenhouse with a Plastic Bag Easy

Materials Needed

  • A small potted plant
  • A transparent plastic bag (big enough)
  • A sunny windowsill

Procedure

  1. Water the plant.
  2. Tie the plastic bag over the plant + pot.
  3. Place in the sun for a few hours.
  4. Look inside the bag — droplets condense on the inside!

What It Teaches

Plants release water vapour (transpiration). It condenses on the bag — same water cycle, in miniature. Plants are part of the cycle too!
